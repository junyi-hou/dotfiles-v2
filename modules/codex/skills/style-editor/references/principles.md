# Academic and Analytical Writing Style Principles

These principles distill the requested references. Use them as guidance, not as text to quote or reproduce.

## Sources

- William Zinsser, `On Writing Well`: https://dn790000.ca.archive.org/0/items/OnWritingWell/on-writing-well.pdf
- William Strunk Jr. and E. B. White, `The Elements of Style`: https://www.jlakes.org/ch/web/The-elements-of-style.pdf
- John H. Cochrane, `Writing Tips for Ph.D. Students`: https://static1.squarespace.com/static/5e6033a4ea02d801f37e15bb/t/5eda74919c44fa5f87452697/1591374993570/phd_paper_writing.pdf

## Zinsser-Derived Principles

- Simplicity is not shallowness. Cut clutter so the reader can see the idea.
- Rewriting is the work. Expect to reshape sentences, not just proofread them.
- Unity matters at every level: one main purpose for the paper, one job for each section, one point per paragraph.
- Respect the reader's attention. Remove warm-up sentences, abstractions, and needless background before the point.
- Keep the author's voice, but make it sound like a clear thinker rather than an academic persona.
- Choose words for accuracy, rhythm, and plain force. Prefer useful nouns and verbs over stacked modifiers.

## Strunk-and-White-Derived Principles

- Use a deliberate structure and hold to it.
- Treat the paragraph as the unit of composition.
- Prefer active voice when it clarifies agency.
- State ideas positively where possible.
- Use definite, specific, concrete language.
- Omit needless words.
- Express parallel ideas in parallel form.
- Keep related words together.
- Put emphasis where readers will feel it, often near the end of the sentence or paragraph.
- Prefer standard, clear usage over novelty.

## Cochrane-Derived Academic Principles

- Identify the one central finding, contribution, recommendation, or decision-relevant takeaway before editing.
- Put the punchline early. Academic and business readers skim and need the result before the full machinery.
- Abstracts should communicate the contribution concretely, not describe the activity of researching.
- Introductions, executive summaries, and memos should start with what the document shows, recommends, or changes, not with broad philosophy, literature throat-clearing, market boilerplate, or public-importance boilerplate.
- Avoid travelogue structure. Do not narrate the author's search process unless method history is itself the contribution.
- Literature review, market context, and background should serve the argument: position the finding, do not recite everything adjacent.
- Robustness, extensions, caveats, and implementation details should support the main result rather than bury it.

## Business and Analytical Adaptation

- Translate "contribution" into the document's real job: key takeaway, recommendation, risk, decision, implication, or action.
- For analytic reports, lead with the most decision-relevant result, then explain evidence, method, uncertainty, and caveats.
- For strategy or business memos, make the ask, recommendation, or tradeoff visible before background detail.
- For status or operating docs, separate signal from chronology. Readers need what changed, why it matters, and what happens next.
- Keep executive prose concrete: name metrics, customers, costs, timelines, constraints, owners, and risks when the source text provides them.
- Do not convert analysis into sales copy. Business writing should be direct and useful, not inflated.

## Revision Passes

1. Key-takeaway pass: name the central finding, claim, recommendation, or contribution and ensure it appears early.
2. Structure pass: check whether headings, paragraphs, and topic sentences support that takeaway.
3. Sentence pass: cut clutter, split overloaded sentences, repair agency, and sharpen nouns and verbs.
4. Risk pass: restore necessary caveats, technical precision, citation integrity, business constraints, and disciplinary conventions.
5. Format pass: ensure Markdown, LaTeX, citations, equations, labels, tables, and references remain valid.

## Common Rewrites

- Replace process language with result language when supported.
- Replace broad openings with direct statements of the paper's object, intervention, or finding.
- Replace broad business openings with direct statements of the finding, recommendation, risk, or decision.
- Replace "There are many important questions concerning X" with the specific question the paper answers.
- Replace "The purpose of this section is to discuss" with the section's substantive claim.
- Replace "This report analyzes X" with "X changed by Y because Z" when the evidence supports it.
- Replace serial hedges with one accurate scope marker.
- Replace noun stacks with clearer relationships.
- Replace long citation dumps with a sentence that explains why the cited work matters.
